Autoregressive Waveform Models

In 2016 WaveNet did the thing everyone thought was too expensive: it modelled raw audio one sample at a time. This part unpacks how — the factorisation that makes it exact, the dilated-convolution stack that gives it memory, the gated residual block at its heart, and the μ-law softmax at its mouth — and confronts the price of doing it: one strictly-ordered forward pass per sample. 1Autoregression — modelling a waveform one sample at a time

Any joint distribution over a sequence can be factored, exactly and without approximation, into a product of conditionals by the chain rule of probability. An autoregressive (AR) model simply parameterises each factor with a neural network and reads them left to right in time.

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} p\big(x_n \mid x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}\big) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} p_\theta\big(x_n \mid x_{

Every sample $x_n$ is predicted from the samples before it, and only those — the model never peeks at the future. Because the factorisation is exact, training is exact too: maximise the log-likelihood of real audio, which for a sequence is just the sum of the per-sample log-probabilities. There is no adversary, no lower bound, no reconstruction surrogate — only a single, honest number to climb.

Teacher forcing — why training is fast but sampling is not

During training the true $x_{every conditional can be evaluated in parallel across the whole clip in one forward pass — this is teacher forcing. Generation is the opposite: the model's own outputs become its next inputs, so the $N$ conditionals must be evaluated in sequence. That asymmetry — parallel to train, serial to sample — is the defining tension of the entire family, and Section 6 measures its cost.

Lineage. WaveNet is the audio member of a family born in vision: van den Oord's PixelRNN / PixelCNN modelled an image pixel-by-pixel with exactly this factorisation and a categorical output. WaveNet transplanted the idea to the time axis and swapped the masked 2-D convolutions for dilated causal 1-D ones. The softmax-over-intensities trick came straight across too.

2Dilated causal convolutions — memory without recurrence

To keep the pitch of a voice stable, the prediction of $x_n$ must depend on samples thousands back — a full pitch period and more. WaveNet buys that reach without an RNN by stacking dilated causal convolutions: the kernel taps skip over a gap that doubles every layer.

Causal means the convolution at time $n$ reads only $x_{\le n}$ (implemented by left-padding), so the AR ordering is never violated. Dilated means layer $\ell$ spaces its $k$ taps $d_\ell = 2^{\ell}$ samples apart. Stacking $L$ such layers with kernel $k=2$ gives a receptive field that grows exponentially in depth while the compute grows only linearly:

$$r = 1 + \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}(k-1)\,2^{\ell} = 1 + (k-1)\big(2^{L}-1\big) \;\xrightarrow{k=2}\; 2^{L}$$

Ten layers already reach back $2^{10}=1024$ samples ($64$ ms at 16 kHz); WaveNet repeats several such dilation stacks (each resetting $1,2,4,\dots,512$) to reach hundreds of milliseconds. Part 1's receptive-field playground visualises this dependency cone; here we take it as given and build the block that lives on top of it.

No recurrence, on purpose. SampleRNN (Section 7) reaches the same context with a hierarchy of RNNs; WaveNet's convolutional route is fully parallel at train time and has no hidden-state bottleneck — the trade is a fixed, finite receptive field rather than an unbounded one.

3The residual block — gated activations, skips, and conditioning

WaveNet is a stack of identical residual blocks. Each one takes the residual stream, runs a dilated causal convolution, passes it through a gated activation unit, and splits the result two ways: back into the residual stream, and out to a global skip sum. The gated activation unit

Instead of a ReLU, WaveNet borrows the multiplicative gate of the LSTM (via PixelCNN): a $\tanh$ "content" branch is modulated element-wise by a $\sigma$ "gate" branch, both fed by their own convolution of the same input.

$$\mathbf{z} = \tanh\!\big(W_{f,k}\!*\mathbf{x}\big)\;\odot\;\sigma\!\big(W_{g,k}\!*\mathbf{x}\big)$$

The sigmoid, bounded in $(0,1)$, acts as a learned valve deciding how much of each $\tanh$ feature survives — empirically this outperformed a plain rectifier for modelling audio. The gated output is then projected by a $1\times1$ convolution (a per-timestep linear layer) into two tensors: one added back to the block input (the residual), one accumulated into the skip path. After the final block all skip contributions are summed and passed through ReLU · 1×1 · ReLU · 1×1 · softmax.

Conditioning — how a waveform model becomes a voice

An unconditioned WaveNet babbles plausible-but-meaningless speech. To say words, the gate is conditioned on side information $\mathbf{h}$. WaveNet distinguishes two kinds:

Global conditioning. A single vector for the whole clip — e.g. a speaker embedding. It is broadcast to every timestep: it biases who is speaking, the timbre and register.
Local conditioning. A time series $\mathbf{h}_n$ — e.g. linguistic features or a mel spectrogram — upsampled to the audio rate. It steers what is said, moment to moment. This is the hook that makes WaveNet a neural vocoder (Part 3).
$$\mathbf{z} = \tanh\!\big(W_{f,k}\!*\mathbf{x} + V_{f,k}\!*\mathbf{h}\big)\;\odot\;\sigma\!\big(W_{g,k}\!*\mathbf{x} + V_{g,k}\!*\mathbf{h}\big)$$
why residual + skip, and not just a deep stack

Two connections do two jobs. The residual path (adding the block's output back to its input) is the He-et-al. identity shortcut: it keeps gradients healthy through 30–40 layers so the deep dilation stack is trainable at all. The skip path is a read-out: every block writes a summand into a shared bus that feeds the output head, so the final prediction can draw on features from every dilation scale at once — fine pitch structure from the shallow, low-dilation layers and slow prosody from the deep, high-dilation ones. Residual carries information forward through depth; skip carries it sideways to the output.

4The output — a softmax over μ-law levels

WaveNet does not regress a real-valued amplitude; it classifies. The next sample is drawn from a categorical distribution over a finite alphabet of levels — a choice that lets the model represent arbitrarily multi-modal, skewed conditionals instead of being pinned to a single Gaussian mean.

Raw 16-bit audio has 65,536 levels — a hopeless softmax. WaveNet first companded the signal with the telephony μ-law curve and quantised to just 256 levels (8 bits). Because the curve is steep near zero, the ear's logarithmic loudness sensitivity is respected and the quiet detail — where most of speech lives — survives the coarse quantisation (Part 1 derives the constant-SQNR property).

$$F(x) = \operatorname{sign}(x)\,\frac{\ln\!\left(1 + \mu\,|x|\right)}{\ln(1+\mu)},\qquad \mu = 255,\quad x\in[-1,1]$$

So the head emits a 256-way softmax; the loss is ordinary cross-entropy against the μ-law bin of the true sample. It is worth pausing on what this buys: the conditional $p_\theta(x_n\mid x_{bimodal — "this could be the top of the pitch period or the start of a new one" — something a mean-squared-error regressor can never express without averaging the two into mush.

Later refinements. The 256-way softmax ignores the ordinal structure of amplitude (bin 130 is "near" bin 131). WaveRNN and the follow-up WaveNets replaced it with a discretised mixture of logistics or a dual 8-bit (coarse+fine) split, cutting the output width while restoring ordinality. The categorical idea, though, is the through-line.

5Sampling — sequential, stochastic, and prone to drift

Here is autoregression made audible. The sampler below seeds a few cycles of a tone, then generates every subsequent sample in order: a low-order linear predictor proposes a mean, and the next sample is a temperature-controlled categorical draw over 64 μ-law bins centred on it. Then it is fed back in and the loop repeats — exactly WaveNet's generation loop, in miniature.

Temperature 0 = greedy (a rock-steady pitch); raise it and the categorical draw takes over until the tone dissolves into noise.

Temperature is the softmax sharpness. At $T=0$ the draw collapses to the predictor's argmax — deterministic, a pure resonator, a stable pitch. As $T$ rises the distribution flattens, draws stray from the prediction, and by $T\!\approx\!1$ the output is broadband noise. This is the same knob that trades a language model's coherence for its creativity; in audio it trades tone for hiss.

Teacher-forcing vs free-running exposes the family's Achilles heel. Free-running (the box unchecked) feeds the model its own outputs, so at any temperature above zero small errors compound — the pitch wanders and can collapse. Tick teacher-forcing and the predictor is fed the clean reference instead: the same temperature now only sprinkles noise on a pitch that never drifts. That gap is exposure bias — the model is trained on ground-truth context it never sees at generation time.

Try it. Set $T\approx0.6$ and toggle the box. Free-running: the tone degrades and destabilises as errors accumulate. Teacher-forcing: rock-steady pitch with a little grain. Same model, same temperature — the only difference is whether the context is honest.

6The cost — exponential context, linear-in-samples generation

Dilation makes context almost free: each added layer doubles the receptive field. But it does nothing for speed. Producing one second of 16 kHz audio requires 16,000 forward passes that cannot be reordered or batched across time — each needs the sample the previous one just made. Slide the depth and watch the asymmetry.

Left: receptive field, doubling per layer. Right: the generation cost — fixed at the sample rate, no matter how deep.
Context is cheap. Receptive field — — reachable from a network only tens of layers deep, because depth buys context exponentially.
Time is not. Generation cost — sequential forward passes — independent of depth. The original WaveNet took minutes to synthesise a second of audio. Escaping this is the story of Parts 3 and 8.

The bottleneck is fundamental to the factorisation, not the architecture: sample $n$ is a function of sample $n-1$, so the $N$ passes form a dependency chain of length $N$. You can make each pass cheaper (WaveRNN), cache its redundant work (Fast WaveNet), or replace the whole serial loop with a parallel model (Parallel WaveNet, GAN vocoders, diffusion) — but you cannot parallelise the chain itself while keeping the exact AR likelihood.

7Escaping the bottleneck — Fast WaveNet to Parallel WaveNet

WaveNet's quality was immediately state of the art and its speed immediately unusable. The two years that followed were a concentrated assault on the sequential cost — three distinct strategies, each trading something for throughput.

MethodIdeaWhat it attacksCost / catch
Fast WaveNet Cache each layer's intermediate activations so a new sample reuses prior convolutions instead of recomputing them. Redundant compute per pass — $O(2^{L})\!\to\!O(L)$ per sample. Still strictly one sample at a time; exact same model, just no wasted work.
SampleRNN A hierarchy of RNNs at different clock rates: slow top tiers set coarse structure, a fast bottom tier emits samples. Long-range context, cheaply — via recurrence instead of deep dilation. Still autoregressive at the sample tier; sequential sampling remains.
WaveRNN A single sparse GRU with a dual (coarse+fine) 8-bit softmax; weight pruning + subscaling to batch and fold the loop. Per-sample compute and memory — small enough to run faster-than-real-time on a phone CPU. Sparsity and subscaling add engineering complexity; still fundamentally AR.
Parallel WaveNet Distil the trained AR "teacher" into an inverse-autoregressive-flow "student" that generates all samples in one parallel pass. The sequential chain itself — sampling becomes $O(1)$ passes. Two-stage training; needs probability density distillation to match the teacher.

Parallel WaveNet & probability density distillation

The cleverest of these deserves a closer look. An inverse autoregressive flow (IAF) can sample in parallel — every output is an invertible transform of an independent noise sample — but it cannot cheaply score arbitrary data, the mirror image of WaveNet's problem. Parallel WaveNet trains an IAF student to imitate the slow AR teacher by minimising the KL divergence between them, evaluated on the student's own samples (which it can score, and the teacher can too):

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(p_{\text{student}}\,\big\|\,p_{\text{teacher}}\big) = \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}\sim p_S}\big[\log p_S(\hat{x}) - \log p_T(\hat{x})\big]$$

The result generates audio faster than real time at the teacher's quality — the pattern of "train a slow exact model, distil into a fast parallel one" that later reappears throughout the field (consistency models, distilled diffusion in Part 8). The GAN vocoders of Part 3 take the more direct route: skip the AR teacher entirely and learn a one-pass generator adversarially.

Read the sequence as a single arc: WaveNet proved raw-audio modelling is possible; Fast WaveNet and WaveRNN made it cheaper without changing the maths; SampleRNN found context through hierarchy; and Parallel WaveNet finally broke the serial chain by distillation. 8Two years that reshaped audio, 2016 → 2018

The autoregressive era of raw-audio synthesis is astonishingly compressed: from the first working model to real-time distillation in barely two years.

2016 · early
PixelRNN / PixelCNN establish autoregressive generation of images with a categorical output and masked convolutions — the direct ancestor of WaveNet. lineage
2016 · Sept
WaveNet (van den Oord et al.) models raw audio sample-by-sample with dilated causal convolutions, gated activations, and a 256-way μ-law softmax — a leap in naturalness for TTS. this part
2016 · late
Fast WaveNet caches convolutional activations, cutting per-sample generation from $O(2^{L})$ to $O(L)$ without changing the model. §7
2017 · Feb
SampleRNN reaches long context with a multi-tier hierarchy of RNNs running at different clock rates. §7
2017 · Nov
Parallel WaveNet distils the AR teacher into an inverse-autoregressive-flow student via probability density distillation — faster-than-real-time, and shipped in Google Assistant. §7
2018
WaveRNN compresses everything into a single sparse GRU with a dual softmax and subscaling — high-quality neural audio on a mobile CPU. The AR vocoder becomes practical. §7
